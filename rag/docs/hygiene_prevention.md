# Hand Hygiene and Everyday Infection Prevention

## Why Hand Hygiene Matters

Hands are the most common vehicle for transferring germs from surfaces to
the eyes, nose and mouth. Proper hand hygiene reduces respiratory infections
such as colds and influenza, gastrointestinal infections, and the spread of
antimicrobial-resistant organisms in both community and healthcare settings.

## Correct Handwashing Technique

1. Wet hands with clean running water (warm or cold).
2. Apply soap and lather by rubbing palms together.
3. Scrub for at least 20 seconds, covering the backs of the hands, between
   the fingers, under the nails, and the thumbs.
4. Rinse thoroughly under running water.
5. Dry with a clean towel or air-dry.

Twenty seconds is roughly the time needed to hum the "Happy Birthday" song
twice. Antibacterial soap is not required; ordinary soap works by lifting
germs and dirt off the skin mechanically.

## Hand Sanitiser

Alcohol-based hand rubs containing at least 60 percent alcohol are an
acceptable substitute when soap and water are unavailable. Apply enough gel
to cover all hand surfaces and rub until dry, which takes about 20 to 30
seconds. Sanitiser is less effective than washing when hands are visibly
dirty or greasy, and it does not reliably inactivate certain stomach
viruses such as norovirus.

## Respiratory Etiquette

Cough or sneeze into a tissue or the inner elbow, never into bare hands.
Dispose of tissues immediately and wash hands afterwards. Wearing a
well-fitting mask in crowded indoor spaces during respiratory illness
seasons further reduces droplet transmission.

## Surface Cleaning

Frequently touched surfaces such as door handles, light switches, phones,
keyboards and toys should be cleaned regularly with household detergent and
disinfected when someone in the household is ill. Bleach-based disinfectants
are effective against most respiratory and gastrointestinal viruses when
used according to the label.

## When Hygiene Alone Is Not Enough

Hand hygiene interrupts transmission but does not replace vaccination where
vaccines exist, nor medical evaluation when symptoms are severe. Fever with
difficulty breathing, symptoms lasting beyond ten days, or dehydration in
young children always warrant professional assessment.
